## Choosing a clinic

**Q: How should I choose a clinic in Korea?**
A: Compare more than price. Look at the doctor's specialty and experience with your specific procedure, whether consultations are done by the operating doctor (not only a coordinator), how complications and revisions are handled, and whether foreign patient support is available. Be cautious of very low prices, pressure to book immediately, or unclear information about who will perform the surgery.

**Q: Are reviews and before-and-after photos reliable?**
A: They can help, but they may be selected or edited, and some reviews may be sponsored. Use them as one input alongside a careful consultation.

## Verifying licenses

**Q: How can I verify that the clinic and doctor are licensed?**
A: Ask for the doctor's full name and specialty, and check whether the clinic is registered to receive international patients. Korea has a registration system for medical institutions that attract foreign patients, and board-certified specialists usually display their certification. Verify through official government or professional association sources where possible, as procedures for checking may change over time.

## Interpreters and communication

**Q: Will there be an interpreter?**
A: Many clinics that serve foreigners offer coordinators or interpreters in English, Chinese, Japanese and other languages. Confirm in advance that interpretation is available for the consultation, consent signing and aftercare instructions. Ask for written aftercare in a language you understand.

## Consultation vs procedure day

**Q: Can I have the consultation and procedure on the same day?**
A: For injectables and lasers this is common. For surgery, many patients prefer a separate consultation day to ask questions, review consent forms and confirm the plan without pressure. Online pre-consultations can help, but an in-person examination is usually needed before surgery.

## How long to stay

**Q: How long should I stay in Korea?**
A: It depends on the procedure and varies by individual; your doctor will advise. As a rough guide, many injectable and laser treatments need only 1–3 days, non-incision eyelid surgery about 5–7 days, incisional eyelid or nose surgery about 7–14 days, and facial contouring or body surgery about 2–3 weeks. Add buffer days for follow-up visits and unexpected delays.

## Flying after surgery

**Q: When can I fly home after surgery?**
A: Always discuss flight timing with your surgeon. Swelling, cabin pressure, long periods of sitting and the risk of blood clots are considerations, especially after major surgery or long-haul flights. Many surgeons prefer you to fly only after stitch removal and a post-operative check.

## Insurance

**Q: Will my insurance cover the procedure or complications?**
A: Cosmetic procedures are generally not covered by Korean national health insurance for foreigners or by most standard travel insurance policies, and complications from elective cosmetic procedures are often excluded. Check your policy carefully and ask whether specialised medical travel insurance is available.

## Emergencies

**Q: What numbers should I know in an emergency?**
A: In Korea, 119 is commonly used for fire and medical emergencies (ambulance), and 112 for police. The Korea tourist information hotline 1330 has offered multilingual travel assistance. Please verify current numbers and services before your trip, as they may change. Also save your clinic's after-hours contact and the address of your accommodation in Korean.
