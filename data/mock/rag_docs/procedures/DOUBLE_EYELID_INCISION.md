## Overview

Incisional double eyelid surgery creates a lasting upper eyelid crease through an incision along the planned crease line. It allows the surgeon to remove excess skin, fat or muscle, which makes it more suitable for thicker eyelids, more skin laxity, previous failed suture methods, or when combined with ptosis correction. The result is generally more durable than the non-incision method, with a longer recovery.

**Also known as:** incisional blepharoplasty, full incision double eyelid, 절개법, 쌍꺼풀 절개, 全切双眼皮, 切开双眼皮, 全切開法, 二重切開.

## How it works

The surgeon makes an incision along the planned crease, removes tissue as needed, and then attaches the skin to deeper eyelid structures so a crease forms when the eye opens. The incision is closed with fine stitches, and the scar is hidden within the crease when the eyes are open. Partial-incision variations use a shorter cut. The crease is generally considered long-lasting, though it changes naturally with age.

## Procedure steps

1. **Consultation:** eyelid assessment, crease simulation, and screening for ptosis or asymmetry.
2. **Anaesthesia:** usually local anaesthesia with or without sedation; some clinics use general anaesthesia, especially if combined with other procedures.
3. **Incision and tissue removal:** skin, fat and sometimes muscle are trimmed.
4. **Crease fixation and closure:** the crease is fixed and the skin sutured.
5. **Recovery:** monitoring at the clinic before discharge.

Surgery typically takes about 1–1.5 hours.

## Recovery & downtime

Swelling and bruising are significant in the first 1–2 weeks. Stitches are commonly removed around 5–7 days. Many people feel ready for public settings after about 2 weeks, but the crease may look high and stiff at first; natural results often take 3–6 months, and scars typically mature over 6–12 months. Clinics commonly advise cold compresses for 48–72 hours, sleeping elevated, avoiding makeup near the incision until advised, and avoiding alcohol, smoking, saunas and strenuous exercise for at least 2–4 weeks.

## Common side effects

- Swelling, bruising, tightness and temporary difficulty closing the eyes fully
- Dryness, watering or irritation
- Asymmetry or a crease that looks higher than intended, sometimes requiring revision
- Visible or thickened scarring in some individuals
- Rarely, infection, bleeding or haematoma
- Rarely, over-correction, eyelid drooping, or difficulty closing the eye

Discuss anaesthesia risks and rare serious complications with your surgeon.

## Before your visit

- Stop smoking and review blood thinners and supplements with your doctor in advance.
- Bring sunglasses and plan for at least 7–10 days in Korea to allow stitch removal.
- Discuss flight timing with your surgeon; many recommend flying only after stitch removal and an initial check.
- Arrange a companion if sedation or general anaesthesia is used.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Do I need a full or partial incision, and why?
- Do I also have ptosis that should be corrected at the same time?
- What type of anaesthesia will be used, and who administers it?
- How will you manage scarring, and what is the revision policy?
- When are stitches removed, and when is it safe for me to fly?
